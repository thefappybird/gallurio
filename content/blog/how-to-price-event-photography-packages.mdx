Most photographers price by looking at what the person down the road charges and shaving 10% off it.
That is not pricing. That is guessing with extra steps, and it is why so many working photographers
are busy and broke at the same time.

Here is a method that starts from your numbers instead.

## What should a photography package cost?

Work it out in three passes, in this order. The first gives you a floor, the second gives you a
target, the third tells you whether the market will bear it.

### 1. Find your cost floor

Add up what a single booking actually costs you to deliver:

- **Direct costs** — travel, parking, second shooter, prints, album, gallery hosting, any props or
  rentals.
- **Time**, at a real hourly rate. Not just the shoot. Enquiry emails, the consultation, the
  contract, travel time, culling, editing, delivery, the follow-up. For a wedding this is routinely
  three to four times the hours on the day.
- **A share of your annual overheads** — gear depreciation, insurance, software, website, marketing,
  accounting. Total them for the year, divide by the number of bookings you realistically take.

That sum is your floor. Below it you are paying for the privilege of working.

### 2. Set your target income

Decide what you need to earn in a year, after tax. Divide by the number of bookings you can take
without burning out — and be honest, because that number is smaller than you think. A wedding
photographer shooting every weekend in season is not doing 52 weddings; between editing, admin and
being a person, 20 to 30 is a full year.

Target income ÷ realistic bookings = what each booking needs to contribute. Add the cost floor.
That is your price.

### 3. Check it against the market

Now look at what others charge — but as a sanity check, not a starting point. If your number is far
above the local range, you need a reason a client can see: a distinctive style, a stronger
portfolio, faster delivery, something. If it is far below, you have probably undercounted your
hours.

## The three package structures that work

**Good–better–best (three tiers).** The most common structure, and it works because it changes the
question from "should I book?" to "which one?". Make the middle tier the one you actually want to
sell — most people take it.

**One package plus add-ons.** Simpler to explain and easier to keep straight. Good if your work is
consistent and the variables are extras: a second shooter, an album, an extra hour.

**Hourly plus deliverables.** Suits corporate and event work where the length genuinely varies. Weak
for weddings, where clients want to know the total, not a rate.

## What most people get wrong

- **Pricing the shoot, not the job.** The eight hours on the day are a fraction of the work. If your
  price only covers the shoot, every booking loses money on the editing.
- **Never raising prices.** Costs go up every year. If your prices have not moved in three years,
  you have taken a real-terms pay cut three times.
- **Discounting instead of restructuring.** A discount says your first number was not real. If
  someone cannot afford a package, take something out of it — fewer hours, no album — so the price
  drops for a reason.
- **Hiding the price entirely.** "Enquire for pricing" filters out serious buyers along with the
  tyre-kickers. A starting-from figure on your public page saves everyone a week of email.
- **Forgetting tax.** Set aside the tax portion the day the deposit lands, not in the following
  April.

## How to present prices publicly

Put a starting price on your public page. Not the full menu — a floor, so people can self-select.
Something like "Wedding coverage from ₱45,000" does more filtering work than three paragraphs about
your approach.

Then make the enquiry easy. Every extra step between "I like this" and "I have contacted them" costs
you bookings, and a contact form that just sends an email means you are the system that turns an
enquiry into a booking.
